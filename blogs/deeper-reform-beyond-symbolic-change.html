Education

Deeper Reform Beyond Symbolic Change

30 October 2026 · 6 min read

Nepal’s education sector stands at a crossroads. With the arrival of Minister Sasmit Pokharel, the youngest member of the current cabinet, there is renewed public optimism that long-neglected problems in schooling may finally receive fresh and serious attention. His early policy decisions, such as abolishing examinations up to Grade 5 and ending bridge course classes, have generated widespread enthusiasm. Parents, teachers, and the media have hailed these initiatives as bold, child-friendly reforms that promise to alleviate the pressure on young learners and reduce inequalities. However, as these policies take shape, they raise a crucial question: Are we reforming the education system, or merely rearranging its symptoms?

Inside the Classroom: The Hidden Culture of Control

In my years of conducting ethnographic fieldwork across both urban and rural schools in Nepal, I have spent countless hours inside classrooms, playgrounds, staff rooms, and corridors observing educational practice from the inside out. What I have repeatedly witnessed, especially in government schools and small private schools, is a deeply embedded culture of control; a hidden curriculum where discipline, fear, and rote memorization dominate the learning environment. These realities must shape how we assess the new reforms because no policy can succeed unless it acknowledges what actually happens inside Nepali schools.

In many Nepali classrooms, discipline is equated with silence. To become “gyaani”, students are supposed to sit perfectly still, maintain a rigid posture, and speak only when explicitly permitted. A quiet class is praised, not because learning is occurring, but because order is visible. Once, during a Social Studies lesson, the principal strolled past the open door. At his approach, the transformation was instant: children stiffened, backs straightened, and even their breath seemed to still. But the moment the principal moved on, low whispers and giggles returned. It was clear: discipline here is performance, maintained by authority figures, not an internalized commitment to learning.

This culture of control extends far beyond behaviour. Students are frequently required to copy answers directly from the board, without any opportunity to demonstrate comprehension or application. Teachers often equate memorization with mastery, and punishment, whether physical, psychological, or public, continues to be used as corrective “medicine.” A child once confided to me after being scolded, “If we say the wrong answer, we get in trouble. Better to say nothing.” This comment encapsulates a pervasive learning environment where fear competes with curiosity, and where the primary survival strategy for students is not to engage with ideas but to avoid risk.

“If we say the wrong answer, we get in trouble. Better to say nothing.”

Exam Reform: A Bold Move or a Superficial Solution?

It is into this landscape that Minister Pokharel’s exam reforms have landed. Removing examinations up to Grade 5 has been widely celebrated as a progressive, child-friendly move. On the surface, it appears to lift a burden that many educators, parents, and students alike see as counterproductive for early learners. Yet my fieldwork suggests this policy may be a superficial fix to a much deeper problem. When exams are simply postponed, the pressure merely shifts upward. Next year’s Grade 6 class will suddenly face weeks of written tests, long exercise loads, and the same rote memorization stresses, with little preparation for this new burden.

More fundamentally, the issue is not exams per se; it is Nepal’s exam culture, which is fear-based, memory-driven, and punitive. In the classrooms I visited, exams tested students on copied definitions, rote facts, recited paragraphs, handwriting, and the ability to memorize under stress. Very little assessed comprehension, problem-solving, or creativity. Students often say, “Memorize the definition, or you fail,” and the teachers say, “Just copy what’s in the book.” Or “Memorize it by heart”. These statements illustrate a system that values surface-level recall above all else; hence, deeper understanding might be unevaluated.

Nepal must ask: Should the goal be to eliminate exams entirely, or to redefine assessment itself? The government’s own 100-point reform agenda acknowledges the need for an “alternative evaluation system” to reduce children’s stress. Indeed, rather than dumping tests, exams should be redesigned as tools for learning. They can be time-friendly and skills-based, with projects, group work, and creative presentations. Continuous assessment and regular feedback, rewarding progress and understanding should replace the all-or-nothing end-of-term exam culture. When done properly, a “good exam” is no longer a threat but a guide for both students and teachers.

What True Transformation Requires

To make these reforms meaningful rather than symbolic, they must be paired with deeper structural change. Firstly, we must redefine success in education by broadening our idea of achievement. School success should not be measured only by final exam scores. Curiosity, creativity, community service, and critical thinking deserve recognition too.

Secondly, all teaching and non-teaching school staff need training in child psychology, positive discipline, internal and continuous assessments, and interactive pedagogy, so that fear no longer becomes the foundation of school culture. School management must encourage communication, research-informed practice, and emotional well-being rather than strict obedience.

Thirdly, learning environments should promote interactive and student-centred methods through storytelling, group work, experiments, art, conversation, and practical tasks, instead of endless copying from textbooks.

Lastly, corporal punishment must be fully eradicated. Practices that suppress students’ agency through physical coercion are antithetical to the values of dignity and inclusion that a modern education system should uphold.

Conclusion

If Nepal is serious about reform, it cannot stop at symbolic gestures. Even when policies are well-intended, they cannot succeed without addressing the hidden rules of the classroom. In many of the same schools that will soon feel the effects of these reforms, children continue to learn lessons beyond the curriculum: that obedience is more valued than initiative. While Minister Pokharel’s leadership has created a moment of opportunity and a responsibility actually to turn hopeful headlines into real change, Nepal must choose depth over symbolism. Our children deserve schools where they learn with joy instead of anxiety; where discipline builds character and not silence; where education empowers, not just performs. If the current government truly aims to transform our schools, it must look beyond exam structure and address the realities we’ve seen. Only then can Nepal cultivate learners who are not just disciplined and diligent, but also confident, critical thinkers capable of shaping the nation’s future.
